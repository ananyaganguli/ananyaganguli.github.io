My story, in chapters

I trained as a chemical engineer and then wrote code for three years. Marketing came later, once I noticed I liked explaining the product more than building it.

Chapter one · 2007 to 2014

The engineer

NIT Durgapur, then Mindtree in Bangalore

I studied chemical engineering and then spent three years building user interfaces in HTML, CSS and JavaScript. I still use that grounding every week. When a product team explains their API, I can follow along, and I can build the landing page myself if I need to.

Chapter two · 2014 to 2017

Learning the language of buyers

MICA in Ahmedabad, then Sulekha.com in Chennai

I went back to school for management communication and came out running SEM, SEO and affiliate campaigns side by side. At Sulekha I brought cost per acquisition down 40%, and the work was shortlisted for a Think with Google case study.

Chapter three · 2017 to 2019

Thinking globally at LinkedIn

LinkedIn, Gurgaon

I planned demand gen, vertical and account-based programs with teams in North America, EMEA and APAC. I cross-sold Sales Navigator to existing LinkedIn Marketing Solutions customers in a pilot program, grew its customer base 30% and took usage among them to 86%. I also got LinkedIn India onto page one for 20 target keywords, and our team was named Top Performing Team, APAC.

Chapter four · 2019 to 2020

Coming home to Kolkata

Tata Metaliks, corporate communications

Back in Bengal, I worked with the agencies behind a Tata Group industrial brand. Organic social reach rose 56%, and the company's customer recommendation score reached 8.1 out of 10 for the first time.

Chapter five · 2020 to 2022

A new country and a blank page

CadMakers, Vancouver

I moved to Canada and joined a construction tech startup where I owned the whole marketing budget and reported to the CEO. The first thing that surprised me was preconstruction, the idea that a project gets built digitally before any material hits the ground. I built go-to-market for two SaaS products from scratch and learned construction from the people who do it, through customer interviews and long sessions with the product team.

Settling into a completely new country turned out to be easier than I expected. People were accommodating, and it helped that Vancouver is a strikingly beautiful city to call home.

Chapter six · 2022 to 2026

Making payments make sense

Shift4, formerly Worldline North America

I ran the content calendar, wrote for technical buyers, ghostwrote for executives and owned paid campaigns across Google, LinkedIn and Meta. Sales asked for a fresh set of collateral with every feature release. Over my time there, qualified leads grew 15% year over year and closed leads grew 27%.

Chapter seven · 2026 to now

Building a brand from the first word

Marketing consultant, early-stage AI startup

A friend co-founded an AI startup and asked if I'd help them figure out how to talk about it. The product is a shared thinking workspace for small teams, which sounds simple until you try to explain why someone should switch to it. I've been shaping the positioning and brand voice, and working out how to grow through content instead of paid ads, including making sure it shows up when people ask AI tools for recommendations.

I'm looking for my next full-time role in content, demand gen or growth marketing, ideally on a team where the people writing the content can see how it performs.

How I work

Whether it's a case study, a launch or a paid campaign, I follow the same three steps.

01

Listen first

I talk to the product team, the customer and the people in sales who hear objections every day. The best material is usually something someone mentioned in passing.

02

Find the tension

Every good story has a before and after. I look for the moment a buyer would recognise as their own problem, and build the piece around it.

03

Ship, measure, repeat

I A/B test headlines, landing pages and emails, and track results in GA4, HubSpot, Marketo and Salesforce. Then I put more effort into whatever's working.

Something I believe

I think most B2B case studies are too polite. The customer quote says everything went smoothly, and nobody believes it. I'd rather show the messy middle, the week the integration broke and how the team fixed it, because that's the part a buyer is actually trying to picture.
